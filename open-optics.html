Open Optics

Build or buy the tools to see it yourself

You don't need a research observatory to gather real evidence. Here's how to pick a first telescope, build one from hardware-store parts, and set up a session that actually works.

Telescope Buying Guide DIY Pipe Refractor Sky-Watching Setup
01 — Buying Guide

Aperture beats magnification, every time

The single most important spec on any telescope is aperture — the diameter of the main lens or mirror. It determines how much light the scope gathers, which determines how much detail you can actually see. Ignore "525x zoom!" marketing entirely.

Choosing your first scope

A Dobsonian reflector is the standard first recommendation in amateur astronomy for good reason: it gives you the most aperture per pound spent, in the simplest possible mount (it just sits on the ground and swivels). Refractors are more rugged and need no collimation, but cost more per inch of aperture. Avoid department-store scopes on flimsy tripods advertising huge magnification numbers — the mount will shake unusably and the optics won't resolve what the box promises.

BudgetRecommended typeWhat you'll see
Entry (~£150–250) 130–150mm tabletop or small Dobsonian reflector Lunar craters, Saturn's rings, Jupiter's cloud bands and moons, bright deep-sky clusters.
Mid-range (~£300–500) 200mm (8") Dobsonian reflector Sharper planetary detail, dozens of galaxies and nebulae under a dark sky, resolved globular clusters.
Astrophotography-ready (~£600+) Refractor or reflector on a computerized equatorial (GoTo) mount Tracked long exposures — the setup used in the Horizon-Spin Star Tracker test.

Rule of thumb: a good 130mm scope on a stable mount will always outperform a wobbly 300mm-marketing-magnification scope on a cheap tripod.

02 — Build It Yourself

The DIY PVC pipe refractor

A working refracting telescope built from plumbing pipe and two lenses — the same optical principle Galileo used, for the price of a hardware-store trip and a cheap lens kit.

What you need

A long plano-convex objective lens (roughly 500–1000mm focal length), a short-focal-length eyepiece lens (a cheap magnifying-glass or loupe lens works), two lengths of PVC pipe sized so one slides snugly inside the other for focusing, end caps, and black paint or flocking paper for the interior.

  1. Paint the inside of both pipe sections matte black (or line with flocking paper) to kill internal reflections that wash out contrast.
  2. Mount the objective lens in an end cap on the wider, outer pipe — this is the "front" of the scope.
  3. Mount the eyepiece lens in an end cap on the narrower, inner pipe.
  4. Slide the inner pipe into the outer pipe. The distance between the two lenses should roughly equal the objective's focal length — sliding the inner tube in and out is your focuser.
  5. Point at a distant object in daylight first, and slide the inner tube until the image snaps into focus. Only move to night-sky targets once daytime focus works reliably.
  6. Mount the finished tube on a camera tripod via a pipe clamp or hose-clamp bracket for stability — handheld will not hold focus.
03 — Session Setup

Getting a sky-watching session right

Good equipment still fails against a bad session. This is the checklist that actually matters before you point anything at the sky.

Before you go out

  • Check a dark-sky map (light-pollution overlay maps are free online) and pick the darkest site within reach.
  • Check the Moon phase — a bright Moon washes out faint deep-sky targets, so plan planetary/lunar nights and deep-sky nights separately.
  • Bring a red-light torch or headlamp only. White light destroys night-adapted vision, which takes ~20–30 minutes to rebuild.
  • Set your scope outside 20–30 minutes before you start, so the optics reach outside temperature and stop distorting the image.

At the eyepiece

  • Start with your lowest-magnification eyepiece to find and center a target, then increase magnification.
  • On a reflector, check collimation (mirror alignment) before serious sessions — a star test at low power on a bright star will show it instantly if it's off.
  • Use a planetarium app (many are free) to know exactly what's overhead and predict ISS or satellite passes in advance.
  • Let your eyes fully dark-adapt before judging faint targets — what looks empty in minute one often resolves after ten.
