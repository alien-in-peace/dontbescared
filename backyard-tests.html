Backyard Tests

Don't take our word for it. Run the test.

Four experiments you can do with cheap, ordinary equipment. Each one produces a result that only makes sense if Earth is a rotating sphere and orbit is real.

Horizon-Spin Star Tracker 3-Turn Spherical Triangle Dual-Hemisphere Star Trails Eratosthenes Shadow Angle
Test 01

The Horizon-Spin Star Tracker

Camera + motorized equatorial mount Clear night, ~1 hour Budget: medium

An equatorial mount tracks the sky by rotating on an axis aligned with Earth's own axis, cancelling out Earth's rotation so stars stay pinpoint-sharp over a long exposure.

  1. Polar-align the mount to true north (or south) using its polar scope or a phone compass + inclination app.
  2. Frame a wide shot that includes both open sky and a visible horizon element — a rooftop, tree line, or fence.
  3. Start tracking and begin a long exposure (2–5 minutes) or an intervalometer sequence for a time-lapse.
  4. Compare the star field to the foreground horizon across the sequence.
Proof: because the mount is cancelling Earth's rotation to lock onto the stars, the foreground horizon visibly drifts and rotates through the frame instead — direct photographic evidence that the ground, not the sky, is the thing moving.
Test 02

The 3-Turn Spherical Triangle

Chalk, string, globe 10 minutes Budget: free

On a flat plane, a triangle's interior angles always sum to 180°. On a curved surface, they don't have to — and that difference is measurable with string and chalk.

  1. On flat paper, draw a triangle where you turn 90° at each of three corners; the interior angles sum to exactly 180°, as expected.
  2. On a globe (or large ball), start at the equator, travel a quarter of the way around to the pole, turn 90°, travel back down to the equator, turn 90° again, and travel along the equator back to your start, turning 90° once more.
  3. Add up the three turns you just made.
Proof: on the sphere, those three 90° turns sum to 270°, not 180° — a closed triangle with angles that only add up correctly if the surface you drew it on is curved.
Test 03

Dual-Hemisphere Star Trails

Fixed tripod DSLR/mirrorless 30–60 min exposure Budget: low

Point a camera on a fixed (non-tracking) tripod at the pole star region and let a long exposure record how stars trail across the sky over time.

  1. In the northern hemisphere, frame the camera on Polaris; in the southern hemisphere, frame it on the region near Sigma Octantis.
  2. Set a long single exposure or stack a sequence of 30-second frames with an intervalometer, then combine them.
  3. Compare trail directions between a northern-hemisphere shot and a southern-hemisphere shot (your own, a friend's, or a published one from each).
Proof: stars trail counter-clockwise around Polaris in the north and clockwise around Sigma Octantis in the south — opposite rotation direction from each hemisphere, exactly what you'd expect looking at a spinning sphere from its two opposite ends.
Test 04

Eratosthenes Shadow Angle Test

2 sticks, tape measure, a phone call Solar noon Budget: free

The original circumference-of-Earth experiment, run by Eratosthenes around 240 BCE — and still reproducible today with a friend a few hundred miles north or south of you.

  1. Find a friend (or another site's data online) several hundred miles due north or south of you.
  2. At solar noon in both locations, plant a stick vertically in the ground and measure its height and the length of its shadow.
  3. Calculate the shadow angle at each location using basic trigonometry (arctangent of shadow length over stick height).
  4. Compare the two angles and the known north-south distance between the two sites.
Proof: the two locations cast shadows at measurably different angles at the exact same moment in time — impossible if the sun's rays were hitting a flat surface uniformly, but exactly what curvature predicts. Plug the angle difference and distance into Eratosthenes' original formula and you'll land within a few percent of Earth's actual circumference.
